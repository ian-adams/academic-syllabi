Course Description

“One of the beautiful things about science is that it allows us to bumble along, getting it wrong time after time, and feel perfectly fine as long as we learn something each time.”

— Martin A. Schwartz (2008), “The Importance of Stupidity in Scientific Research”

Criminal law is a constant source of fascination. In addition to its prominent place on television networks’ schedules, criminal law governs the use of the most important form of governmental power: the power of government to incarcerate – and even execute. All forms of law govern human behavior, but criminal law governs the most basic questions of how we treat one another and defines those forms of human action which society will not tolerate. Criminal law also presents a number of questions central to political institutions, including the Executive and Judicial Branches, and the Bureaucracy. This class is intended as much as an examination of these institutions and how they function within the context of criminal justice. This class will not teach you the skills needed to serve as a criminal defense attorney (least of all, for yourself), but it will engage you in a critical and engaged examination of our duties to others and the state’s role in regulating individuals’ actions.

Prerequisites

Course Learning Outcomes

After successful completion of this course, you will be able to:

  1. Explain the purposes, sources, and functions of American criminal law, and identify the constitutional limits on the power to define and punish crime.
  2. Identify the elements of a criminal offense – actus reus, mens rea, concurrence, and causation – and apply them to a given set of facts.
  3. Distinguish among the inchoate offenses of attempt, conspiracy, and solicitation, and explain the doctrines governing accomplice and vicarious liability.
  4. Differentiate justification defenses from excuse defenses, and evaluate which, if any, applies to a given case.
  5. Analyze the elements of the major offense categories, including homicide, offenses against the person, offenses against property and habitation, white-collar crime, offenses against public order and morality, and offenses against the state.
  6. Evaluate competing arguments about the purposes of punishment and the design of sentencing systems.
  7. Assess the reliability and credibility of legal and factual sources, including AI-generated content, and use them with proper attribution.
  8. Communicate clearly and precisely about criminal law concepts, cases, and controversies.

The learning outcomes are equivalent to those of a face-to-face (F2F) version of the course.

Instructional Methods

This is a fully online, asynchronous course. There are no scheduled meetings, and there is nothing you must attend at a set time. It requires the same level of commitment and engagement as a face-to-face class, and you should expect to spend roughly the same total time and effort per credit hour that you would in a traditional section.

Asynchronous does not mean self-paced. This is not an independent study. You have firm weekly deadlines, and work cannot be saved up for the final weeks of the semester. Each week’s material builds on the last; if you fall behind, catching up is difficult and the structure of the course will work against you.

The course runs on a Monday-to-Sunday week. New material opens Monday; that week’s work is due the following Sunday at 11:59 p.m. Eastern. There are exactly two exceptions, both at the end of the semester: nothing is due during Thanksgiving recess, and the last module is due on Friday, December 4. Both are marked in the schedule. All course content is delivered through two places, and two places only:

  • Blackboard, which is the front door for everything. Announcements, the syllabus, the final exam, and every link into the textbook platform live here.
  • SAGE Vantage, the interactive textbook platform, which you reach by clicking through Blackboard (see the warning under Required Materials).

Each weekly module in Vantage is built from the same set of components:

  • Self-Assessments, which check what you already know before you read;
  • Reading Activities, most of them “You Decide” case problems that ask you to resolve a real appellate case on its facts;
  • Knowledge Checks, short section-by-section comprehension checks with unlimited attempts;
  • Critical Thinking Checks, which ask you to apply the chapter’s doctrine rather than recall it; and
  • a Chapter Quiz, which is your one graded, single-attempt assessment for the week.

To succeed in this course, you must be self-motivated and organized. Set specific days and times each week to do the work, and treat them as you would a class meeting.

Assignments and Assessments

All coursework and rubrics are available through Blackboard. All assignments are due, and the final exam is administered, on the dates in the course schedule below. All quizzes and the final exam are administered and secured within the Vantage and Blackboard platforms, with questions drawn randomly from large test banks so that no two students see an identical assessment.

This course asks you to do real work every week. There is no midterm paper and no final essay this semester; instead, the weekly Vantage modules carry most of the load, and a comprehensive, timed final exam closes the course on December 7.

Weekly Vantage Work

Every week you complete one chapter module in Vantage (two in the first and last weeks). Each module contains a mix of the following:

  • Self-Assessments and Reading Activities, including the “You Decide” case problems. These are assigned for completion with unlimited attempts: you earn credit by doing the work and engaging with it, not by getting a particular answer on the first try.
  • Knowledge Checks. Short, graded comprehension checks tied to each section of the chapter. You have unlimited attempts, so there is no good reason to score poorly on these. Use them.
  • Critical Thinking Checks. These ask you to apply doctrine to new facts rather than recall a definition. They are assigned for completion.
  • Chapter Quiz. Fifteen questions, graded, and one attempt only. This is the assessment that actually tests whether you learned the chapter, and its questions closely resemble what you will see on the final exam. Do the rest of the module before you open the quiz.

Everything in a given week is due the same night: Sunday at 11:59 p.m. Eastern – except the final module, Chapter 16, which is due Friday, December 4. Please note that the time required varies by chapter. If you have not read the chapter first, these activities will take considerably longer than they are designed to take.

Final Exam

The final exam is comprehensive, covering all sixteen chapters. It consists of 120 questions worth 5 points each, drawn from substantial test banks, and it is administered and secured in Blackboard – not in Vantage. You have one attempt.

This is the single heaviest item in the course – see the grading weights below. Treat it accordingly.

Read these details carefully. This exam runs on a single morning, and it is timed.

  • Date: Monday, December 7, 2026 – this date only.
  • Exam opens: 8:00 a.m. Eastern
  • Exam closes: 11:59 a.m. Eastern
  • Time limit: 120 minutes from the moment you begin.
  • Latest start to receive the full 120 minutes: 9:59 a.m. Eastern.

The window is open for just under four hours, but your personal timer is 120 minutes. If you begin after 9:59 a.m., the exam still closes at 11:59 a.m. and you will get less than the full time. Start at 8:00 a.m. if you possibly can. Do not plan to start at 11:00.

Understand exactly what happens if you run out of time. The exam does not pause, and it does not extend. When your timer expires or the window closes – whichever comes first – it submits automatically with the grade you have earned to that point, and every question you did not answer is scored as a zero. If you are halfway through when it closes, you receive credit for half an exam. There is no reopening it, no finishing it later, and no partial-credit appeal. Budget the 120 minutes and keep moving.

The Registrar does not assign an exam slot to asynchronous sections, so this date and window are set by me and stated here as the official time.

If you are outside the Eastern time zone, convert this now and put it in your calendar. 8:00 a.m. Eastern is 5:00 a.m. Pacific. It is your responsibility to sit the exam during the stated Eastern-time window regardless of where you are. If that window is genuinely impossible for you – a documented conflict, a deployment, a time zone that makes it untenable – email me within the first two weeks of the semester so we can arrange something. Do not wait until December.

The final exam has electronic proctoring enabled to ensure ethical test taking. I strongly encourage you to use University of South Carolina technology – on-campus computers and WiFi – when completing the final exam. I cannot and will not be responsible for claimed technology failures on personal equipment. Given the timer, test your setup in advance.

There will absolutely be no assignments, quizzes, or exams accepted after 11:59 a.m. on December 7, 2026. None. Add it to your calendar now. Failure to submit any assignment, quiz, or exam by that date and time will automatically be counted as a zero toward your final grade. I will accept no appeals on this, so please plan ahead.

Grade Grubbing

“Grade grubbing” is the act of trying to get a higher grade on an assignment or test by pestering the instructor or using dishonest methods. It often takes the form of repeatedly asking for extra credit or trying to talk an instructor into changing a grade. Students do it because they are afraid of a low grade, or because they have become overly focused on grades as such.

You should avoid this at all costs. It will not help you in this course. It is stressful, time-consuming, and it usually ends in frustration for everyone. Focus instead on understanding the material and doing your best on each module. That will improve your grades, and it will also make the semester worth something to you.

Academic Integrity

When students, faculty, and staff wrote the Carolinian Creed, their choice for the first tenet was, “I will practice personal and academic integrity.” As partners in your learning, it matters to both of us that what you submit is a genuine reflection of your own work and understanding.

You are expected to practice the highest standards of academic integrity. Any deviation will result in a referral to the Office of Student Conduct and Academic Integrity (OSCAI) for an alleged Honor Code violation. If a violation is found, there is a minimum academic penalty of failing the assignment, in addition to whatever outcome OSCAI reaches. This includes improper citation of sources, unauthorized use of artificial intelligence, and any other form of academic misrepresentation.

Suspected violations of Cheating – “unauthorized assistance in connection with any academic work” – and Falsification – “misrepresenting or misleading others with respect to academic work or misrepresenting facts for an academic advantage” – will be referred to OSCAI.

Plagiarism. Using the words or ideas of another as if they were your own is a serious form of academic dishonesty. If another person’s complete sentence, syntax, key words, or specific or unique ideas and information are used, you must give that person credit through proper citation.

A note specific to this course: the Chapter Quizzes and the final exam are single-attempt, closed assessments. Sharing quiz or exam questions with other students, in any forum, is unauthorized assistance. So is receiving them. The unlimited-attempt Knowledge Checks exist precisely so that you have a legitimate, unlimited way to practice – use those instead.

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) Policy

Generative AI tools are permitted in this course in specific contexts and with acknowledgment. Understanding when these tools help and when they mislead is itself a skill this course expects you to build – which is why your very first module, Chapter VM, is devoted to information literacy, academic integrity, and responsible AI use. Take it seriously; it sets the terms for the rest of the semester.

You may use generative AI tools to brainstorm, to check your understanding of a concept, to generate practice questions for yourself, to explain a case you found difficult, or to help you organize your thinking before you write.

You may not use generative AI tools to answer Chapter Quizzes or the final exam. These are closed, single-attempt assessments, and using AI on them is unauthorized assistance under the university’s academic integrity policy. Nor may you submit AI-generated text as your own work anywhere in this course.

When you use these tools on any submitted work, disclose it. State which tool you used, when, and how – whether it generated ideas, phrasing, structure, or text. If AI-generated language appears in what you submit, it must be attributed. Style guides now address this directly; see the guidance from MLA, APA, and Chicago.

Two cautions worth stating plainly, because this is a law course. First, you are fully responsible for everything you submit, including anything an AI tool produced. “The model said so” is not a defense, here or in professional life. Second, general-purpose AI tools are demonstrably unreliable on legal questions. They invent cases, misstate holdings, garble the elements of offenses, and blur the law of one jurisdiction into another. Real lawyers have been sanctioned for filing AI-hallucinated citations. Verify everything against the textbook and the actual case.

If you are unsure whether a particular use is acceptable, ask me before you do it. I would much rather answer that question in advance than adjudicate it afterward.

Expectations

Expectations for Course Behavior

Professionalism is expected at all times, including in your online interactions. Because the university classroom – physical or virtual – is a space designed for the free exchange of ideas, we show respect for one another in all interactions, exhibiting patience and courtesy in our exchanges. Appropriate language and restraint from verbal attacks upon those whose perspectives differ from your own is the minimum requirement.

Criminal law inherently centers on topics about which people hold strong opinions grounded in personal values, beliefs, and interests. This course will ask you not only to acknowledge and respect perspectives that differ from your own, but at times to challenge your own views and step into another’s perspective to test the strengths and limits of your argument. Respectful behavior is the expectation, norm, and requirement in this class. Arguments and ideas may – and should – be challenged. Personal attacks, disrespectful behavior, and hateful attitudes toward people or groups of people will not be.

Netiquette

Written communication in an online course carries all the weight that tone of voice and body language would carry in person, so it deserves care:

  • Respect one another. Avoid personal attacks, even in sharp disagreement.
  • Avoid ALL CAPS. It reads as shouting.
  • Use proper salutations. Begin with a polite greeting. “Hey” is not one.
  • Use a clear subject line, and include the course number (CRJU 314). Sign off with your full name.
  • Use correct grammar and spelling. Text-message shorthand is not appropriate.
  • Communicate tastefully. No profanity or offensive language.
  • Reread before you send. Always.

  • I will respond to email in a timely fashion – within 24 to 72 hours, not including weekends and holidays.
